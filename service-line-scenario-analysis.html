Isolating the rate lever from the service-line bet

A development sponsor had two open underwriting questions and one pro forma. Separating them changed which one they funded first.

The situation

Before L3P

A development sponsor building a new independent living community in a Southwest submarket had two open questions before finalizing the project's underwriting: how aggressively to price against the comp set, and whether to build an in-house home health line into the community from day one.

Both decisions were living inside a single working pro forma, which meant neither one's true economics could be seen on its own — a stronger topline could just as easily be masking a drag from the other.

What L3P did

The build

Built four parallel five-year pro formas off one shared architecture — a base case, a rate-optimized case, and a home health overlay of each — then layered a variance-decomposition pass on top that holds occupancy and expense methodology identical within each pair, so every dollar of difference could be attributed to a single lever rather than blended into one topline delta.

For the home health line specifically, built a standalone unit-economics model with sixty months of lease-up detail and a two-variable sensitivity grid — client penetration against average care hours per client per week — to find the exact operating conditions where the line stops being a drag.

THE FINDING
Never turns positive at the planned service level

At the sponsor's underwritten assumption — 8 hours of care per client per week — Year-5 operating income for the home health line stays negative even at 40% resident penetration, double the stabilized case being underwritten. The line only crosses into profitability once average care hours climb into the 12–16 per week range alongside higher penetration, a service-delivery decision, not a lease-up one.

Read down the 8-hour column — the sponsor's actual assumption — and the line is negative at every penetration level tested, including double the underwritten case. Read across the 20% row and it only turns around once care hours nearly double. That's a different capital conversation than "wait for lease-up."

What changed

Decision

The rate strategy moved forward as the primary lever — it was the highest-confidence dollar in the model and didn't depend on a second, unproven line of business. The home health build was reframed from a lease-up timing question to a service-delivery design question: the sponsor now has the specific care-hours threshold the model needs to clear before the line is worth underwriting into the base case, rather than adding it as a growth assumption and finding out in year three.
