Case Study
Recurring Revenue · Multi-Site Operations

The Week That Wasn't Behind

A multi-site operator in a recurring-revenue services business was reading a mid-month pacing report as a demand problem. It was a cash-posting problem — and the reaction it was driving was doing more damage than the number itself.

16%
Of a roughly $5M annual revenue base, only about 16% was cash-visible by the mid-month pacing checkpoint — regardless of how the month was actually performing.
The Situation

Leadership tracked performance against budget through a weekly pacing report. At the mid-month checkpoint, the report showed revenue running well short of plan. Read at face value, that looked like a demand problem — and it was treated like one: staffing levels were pulled back, and pricing concessions went out to try to close the gap before month-end.

The pacing report was never wrong about what had posted. It was wrong about what that meant.

What the Review Found

A full trailing-twelve-month cash-posting review showed the business's revenue is structurally back-loaded — a pattern of the underlying billing cycle, not a monthly anomaly. Roughly 64.5% of gross revenue posts in the final third of the month (days 22–31), driven by when charges actually hit the ledger, not when the underlying activity occurred.

Before

Pacing reports benchmarked every checkpoint against a straight-line 50%-by-mid-month expectation. Any week-two read below that line was flagged as behind — and acted on immediately.

After

Pacing benchmarks rebuilt against the business's actual cash-posting curve. A week-two read is now judged against what should realistically be visible by then — not an assumption that doesn't match how the revenue actually lands.

Method
  1. Reconstructed the posting curvePulled a full trailing-twelve-month, transaction-level cash-posting history and mapped every dollar to its actual day-of-month posting date — not a batch estimate.
  2. Isolated the pattern from the noiseBucketed postings into weekly windows across all twelve months to confirm the back-loading was a consistent structural pattern, not a one-off month or a reporting artifact.
  3. Rebuilt the benchmarkReplaced the straight-line pacing assumption with checkpoint targets calibrated to the actual posting curve, so each weekly read could be judged against a realistic expectation.
  4. Reframed the response protocolEstablished that a below-benchmark early-month read is not, by itself, a signal to act — removing the false-alarm trigger that had been driving reactive staffing and pricing decisions before the month had a chance to post.

The finding didn't just correct a report — it stopped a pattern of reactive decisions being made against a number that was never going to be complete at the point it was being judged. Staffing and pricing moves triggered by a mid-month read are decisions made against roughly a sixth of the month's actual revenue. Recalibrating what "on pace" looks like removed the false signal without changing anything about how the business was actually performing.
