Price-vs-Volume Decomposition · Senior Living

The Growth Number That Was Actually Three Different Stories

A regional operator managing a portfolio of licensed, resident-based properties — billing revenue per occupied unit across several service tiers — read a rising top line as one broad, healthy growth story. It wasn't.

90%
Of one tier's revenue growth was driven almost entirely by rate, not volume — while the portfolio's blended read showed a far more even, and far more misleading, 57% volume / 43% price split.
The Situation

Total revenue was up, and at a glance that looked like confirmation the whole portfolio was moving in the same direction. But a single blended growth number is an average — and averages can make three very different underlying stories look like one moderate one.

What the Model Found

At the blended level, portfolio revenue growth split roughly 57% from added units and 43% from higher rates — a reasonable-looking, broad-based story. Segmented by service tier, that even split turned out to be an average of three tiers that looked nothing alike: one grew almost entirely on volume with rate essentially flat, a second grew almost entirely on price while unit counts barely moved, and a third lost ground on both fronts at once. None of the three individual stories resembled the blended number that would have been reported up.

Blended read

Portfolio revenue is up, split roughly 57% volume / 43% price — reads as one moderate, broad-based growth story across the whole book.

Segmented read

One tier's growth is 97% volume, a second's is 90% price, and a third is shrinking on both. The blended split was an average of three stories — not a description of any one of them.

Method
  1. Pulled unit volume and revenue by tierGathered occupied-unit counts and dollar revenue for each service tier over a multi-month window, from the same operational systems that feed the monthly close.
  2. Split the change into two questionsFor each tier, separated the revenue change into how much came from more units and how much came from a higher rate paid per unit.
  3. Tested both answers against the dollar movementReconciled the two effects, tier by tier, until they accounted for the entire revenue change with nothing left over.
  4. Rebuilt the blended number from the piecesRolled the segmented findings back up to the portfolio level to show exactly what the single blended split had been hiding.

The blended split leadership would have seen without segmentation

The finding reframes how growth gets reported to ownership and capital partners: a single blended volume/price split is a summary statistic, not a diagnosis. It can average a tier that's overheating on price together with a tier that's quietly losing units and produce something that reads as moderate and safe. Segmenting first and blending second is the only way to catch that — and it's the same reconciliation discipline applied at a different level of granularity, repeatable on any multi-tier revenue base.
