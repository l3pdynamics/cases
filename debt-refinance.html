Right-Sizing a Deal That Was About to Cost More Than It Should

An operator was moving through a refinance with two lenders and a debt structure sized against pre-turnaround assumptions. The numbers on the term sheets worked. The property's actual cash flow didn't agree.

$944K
Restructuring the deal cut ownership's projected two-year cash contribution from roughly $1.49M to $546K — without walking away from either lender.
The Situation

The refinance was already in motion when we were brought in — two term sheets on the table, a senior loan and a secondary financing layer, both sized to the original deal assumptions. On paper, the structure closed the transaction. Against the property's actual, current cash flow, it didn't.

Two separate lenders meant two separate sets of covenants — loan-to-value limits, minimum coverage ratios, disbursement triggers — each with its own math and its own tolerance for how thin the numbers could run before the deal stopped qualifying.

What the Review Found

Tested against both lenders' actual covenants, the original structure was carrying more debt than the property's near-term operations could service. Combined debt coverage came in at 0.63x in year two — well below the 1.0x minimum either lender required — which meant ownership was on the hook to personally fund the shortfall out of pocket, not the property.

Before

Secondary financing sized to fully repay the prior note, without testing whether the resulting combined debt service actually fit the property's near-term cash flow. Combined coverage ran at 0.63x against a 1.0x requirement.

After

Secondary financing re-sized to the level the property's cash flow could carry — trading a smaller recovery on the prior note for a structure both lenders' covenants would actually pass, and ownership could actually fund.

Method
  1. Modeled both lenders togetherBuilt a combined covenant model spanning both term sheets — senior debt service, secondary financing, and the combined coverage test each lender required independently.
  2. Tested the original structure against real cash flowRan the property's actual year-one and year-two operating proforma against both sets of covenants, rather than the assumptions the original term sheets were built on.
  3. Built the trade-off tableSized secondary financing at every level from full to zero, showing exactly how much cash ownership would need to fund at each size — and how much of the prior note went unrecovered at each level.
  4. Re-sized to the level that actually cleared covenantsSelected the secondary financing amount that brought combined coverage into a passing range for both lenders while minimizing the cash ownership had to contribute.

The deal didn't need a different lender or a longer negotiation — it needed the secondary financing sized to what the property could actually carry, not what the original assumptions called for. Modeling both lenders' covenants together, instead of negotiating each in isolation, is what surfaced the trade-off and let ownership choose a structure that worked on both sides of the table.
